01The challenge

Design a McDonald's Happy Meal toy that is attractive and cheap, can be injection molded, and can be mass-produced. The memo to the client weighed practicality, sustainability, safety, cost, ease of use, and looks, and the toy had to be safe for children aged 4 to 8, which means nothing small enough to choke on, even after a child takes it apart.

02My contribution

Three-person team with Saina Srivastava and Mariangle Mendez. We brainstormed, scored concepts, modeled in SolidWorks, and 3D-printed prototypes together, and co-wrote the technical memo. For the Duke 2025 Toy Fair, I made the toy's video.

03Process & iteration

Brainstorming started wide; an early favorite was a carrier jet with Toy Story soldiers. We narrowed the field with a weighted Pugh matrix that favored price above everything else, and the plain train won.

We split the train into a head, a chassis, and wheels. The head slides onto the chassis along a horizontal male/female mate, so it can't pop off upward during play but a child can swap it with little force. Shaft collars press onto the axles so the wheels can never come off. Every part extrudes along one axis to keep it injection-moldable, and the head and chassis are solid so they survive drops.

Our first prints didn't fit. The 3D printers hold about 0.1 mm, looser than the fits we had designed, so the head and chassis mated badly. We opened up the tolerances in the CAD for the printed prototypes, and because we had started CAD early, there was time to reprint.

04Outcome & evidence

Every detachable piece passed the choke-tube test in every orientation. Three heads (standard, subway, and bullet) share one mate, so they work as collectibles. In production PETG, the toy comes to $0.31 a unit, with one 100-ton press per head design running 100,000 units a week. We presented it at the Duke 2025 Toy Fair.

The tightest tolerance sits where safety does: the shaft collar bore is ⌀10.11 ±0.01 mm on a ⌀10.16 mm axle, a press fit that keeps the wheels on. The head-to-chassis mate is looser, at ±0.05 mm, because a head that comes off is the point of the toy, not a hazard.

05Reflection

The biggest takeaway was to start early. We finished most of the CAD before spring break, which left time to print, find the tolerance problem, and work around it without lowering the standard of the toy. The snap-fit was the other lesson: one connection idea solved several problems at once and stayed injection-moldable.
